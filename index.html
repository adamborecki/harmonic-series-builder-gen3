When any instrument plays a note, it's actually sounding a whole stack of higher pitches at once, layered quietly on top of the main note. These are called partials (or harmonics/overtones). The 1st partial is the main note itself (the "fundamental"); the 2nd is exactly double its frequency, the 3rd is triple, and so on. This tool has you notate where partials 1–8 land on the staff.

Each partial's ratio to the one right before it: 2:1 (octave), 3:2 (P5), 4:3 (P4), 5:4 (M3), 6:5 (m3), 8:6 = 4:3 (P4 again), 7:8 (M2, going down). That's the same sequence of intervals you'll be counting on the staff, in order.

A half step is the smallest distance between two notes on a piano — one key to the very next key, black or white, with nothing in between. A whole step is two half steps. On the piano at the bottom of the screen, every adjacent key (white-to-black or white-to-white where there's no black key between, like E→F) is a half step.

The seven letter names A–G repeat up the keyboard. A sharp (♯) raises a note one half step; a flat (♭) lowers it one half step; a natural (♮) means neither. This tool separates the two decisions: Step A is "which line or space" (the letter name), Step B is "which accidental" (sharp/flat/natural).

Scientific pitch notation: C4 is middle C, and each octave is numbered starting at C — so B3 sits directly below middle C, and C5 sits an octave above it.

Lines and spaces each represent one letter name, alternating up the staff. The treble clef (top) is for higher notes, the bass clef (bottom) for lower ones — together they form the "grand staff" shown here.

A quick way to remember which letter is which — read bottom to top:

"Every Good Boy Does Fine"

Generic interval (counting on the staff): Count every line and space between the two notes, including the starting note as "1" — don't count from zero. So an octave, even though it's "8 notes away," is counted 1‑2‑3‑4‑5‑6‑7‑8, with the starting note itself as the first count. A third spans 3 letter names, a fifth spans 5, and so on — this only depends on how many lines/spaces you cross, not on any sharps or flats.

Specific interval (counting half steps): Once you know the generic size, you need to know exactly how many half steps make it major, minor, or perfect. These three come up constantly in the harmonic series above — worth memorizing cold:

Minor Third (m3)3 half steps
Major Third (M3)4 half steps
Perfect Fifth (P5)7 half steps
Major Second (M2)2 half steps
Perfect Fourth (P4)5 half steps

The two smaller ones above come up less often here (mainly around the 7th and 8th partials) — good to recognize, lower priority to memorize than the top three.
